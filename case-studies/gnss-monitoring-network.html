Project summary

Build the network and the organization that could sustain it.

Between 2017 and 2021, I directed a program to deploy 25 GPS/GNSS stations across Southeast India for atmospheric monitoring. The scientific purpose depended on dense, continuous observations, but the delivery problem was much broader: identify and align partner sites, procure and evaluate equipment, coordinate field installation, maintain communications, and keep data moving across a distributed network.

I managed a 20-member cross-functional group spanning scientists, engineers, vendors, and government stakeholders. The program was completed on schedule and 10% under its $1M budget, with zero data loss in the operational network.

Challenge

A distributed instrument is only as strong as its weakest handoff.

Every station sat inside a chain of dependencies: physical siting, power, receiver configuration, network connectivity, institutional access, maintenance, and central data handling. Early real-time transmission failures were approximately 50%. That level of fragility could turn a geographically impressive network into an unreliable scientific asset.

The program therefore required simultaneous progress on deployment volume and operating resilience. Adding stations without solving the transmission architecture would multiply failure points rather than build capability.

Constraints

Geography, institutions, vendors, and reliability.

  • Distributed sites: Field and rooftop installations had different access, power, connectivity, and maintenance conditions.
  • Partner coordination: Host organizations needed clear responsibilities, schedules, and escalation paths.
  • Vendor quality: Equipment choices affected cost, uptime, maintainability, and long-term research consistency.
  • Data continuity: Intermittent communications could not be allowed to become permanent data gaps.
  • Budget discipline: Deployment choices had to preserve scientific usefulness while remaining inside public-program constraints.

What I led

Program architecture from procurement to operations.

I translated the scientific objective into a delivery plan covering site onboarding, procurement, installation sequencing, risk management, and operations. I coordinated the multidisciplinary team, managed partner and vendor dependencies, and kept technical decisions connected to schedule and budget consequences.

To address transmission fragility, I led a redesign around store-and-forward redundancy. Data could be retained locally when a link failed and transmitted after connectivity recovered. That changed the operating model from a brittle real-time stream into a resilient pipeline. I also built a vendor evaluation framework that made tradeoffs more explicit and was later adopted department-wide.

Reliability improved when we stopped treating communication failure as an exception and designed the network around the assumption that it would happen.

Outcomes

Scale without sacrificing control.

  • 25 monitoring stations deployed across Southeast India.
  • $1M program delivered on schedule and 10% under budget.
  • Zero data loss across the operational network.
  • Real-time transmission failures reduced from 50% to 2%.
  • Vendor evaluation framework adopted department-wide.

The project demonstrates both delivery scale and operating improvement. The numbers reflect team and system performance; my role was to lead the plan, coordination, architecture decisions, vendor discipline, and recovery work that enabled those outcomes.

Methods

Program and technical approach.

  • Site-readiness assessment and partner responsibility mapping.
  • Deployment sequencing tied to procurement and team capacity.
  • Vendor criteria covering performance, cost, support, and maintainability.
  • Store-and-forward redundancy for intermittent network conditions.
  • Operational monitoring, escalation, and field-maintenance planning.
  • Atmospheric research workflows connecting GNSS observations to water-vapor analysis.

Reflection

What I would add today.

I would instrument the network with a modern observability layer: station-level health, communication latency, data completeness, maintenance history, and automated anomaly alerts in one operating view. I would also formalize total-cost-of-ownership comparisons during procurement so early price, field effort, failure behavior, and support quality were evaluated together.

The durable insight is that infrastructure programs succeed when reliability, ownership, and recovery are designed before scale makes every weakness expensive.
