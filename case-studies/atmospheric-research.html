Project summary

Three questions about the same difficult variable.

Atmospheric water vapor affects weather development but is uneven in space, changes rapidly in time, and is difficult to observe continuously. My research approached that challenge from three linked directions.

GNSS tropospheric tomography examined how a network of ground receivers could reconstruct three-dimensional water-vapor structure. Diurnal IWV variation used three years of integrated-water-vapor observations and harmonic analysis to identify repeatable daily patterns. INSAT-3D / GNSS validation compared satellite-derived estimates with ground-based measurements to understand agreement, bias, and limitations across observing systems.

Combining them here shows the progression from infrastructure and geometry to temporal behavior and cross-platform validation. Each project also has public code so technical visitors can inspect the workflow rather than relying only on a project description.

Challenge

Recover useful structure from sparse and imperfect observations.

GNSS receivers do not measure a complete atmospheric volume directly. They infer delay along paths between ground receivers and satellites. Tomography must convert those intersecting paths into a three-dimensional field, a problem that is sensitive to receiver geometry, data availability, regularization, and validation.

Temporal analysis faces a related issue. Daily water-vapor cycles are mixed with weather events, seasonal changes, missing observations, and noise. Satellite comparison adds differences in sampling, retrieval physics, resolution, and timing. The common challenge is to distinguish a real atmospheric pattern from an artifact of the observing system or analytical method.

Constraints

Geometry, scale, timing, and comparability.

  • Sparse geometry: Tomographic reconstruction depends on the number and arrangement of receivers and satellite paths.
  • Ill-posed inversion: Multiple atmospheric fields may be consistent with limited observations, requiring explicit assumptions and regularization.
  • Temporal variability: Weather and seasonal changes can mask or distort repeatable diurnal structure.
  • Instrument differences: GNSS and satellite products observe the atmosphere through different spatial and temporal lenses.
  • Reproducibility: Data preparation, quality control, analysis choices, and visualization need enough documentation to be reviewed.

What I built

Research workflows that connect observations to an interpretable result.

For tomography, I developed and evaluated a compact receiver configuration, with work indicating that a minimum five-receiver setup could support the intended reconstruction approach under the examined conditions. The workflow connects receiver geometry, slant-path observations, voxel representation, inversion logic, and three-dimensional visualization.

For diurnal variation, I prepared a three-year integrated-water-vapor time series and applied harmonic analysis to test whether daily components were statistically meaningful. The harmonics were significant in 93% of the evaluated periods, providing evidence of repeatable temporal structure while preserving the distinction between a statistical pattern and a deterministic forecast.

For satellite validation, I structured the quality-control, temporal matching, and comparison steps needed to place INSAT-3D estimates alongside GNSS observations. The value was not simply a single correlation figure; it was a reproducible way to inspect agreement and diagnose when differences could arise from timing, resolution, or retrieval behavior.

Scientific confidence came from making the observation chain and analytical assumptions visible, not from producing the smoothest visualization.

Methods

Scientific and technical approach.

  • GNSS delay processing and integrated-water-vapor analysis.
  • Receiver/satellite geometry and voxel-based tomography.
  • Inverse-problem formulation, constraints, and three-dimensional visualization.
  • Time-series quality control and harmonic analysis.
  • Statistical significance testing across temporal periods.
  • Satellite/ground matching, comparison, and diagnostic visualization.
  • Python-based reproducible research workflows.

Reflection

What I would extend now.

I would package the three repositories behind a shared data model and reproducible environment, add automated tests around unit conversion and temporal matching, and create a single interactive explorer linking station geometry, diurnal behavior, and satellite comparison. For tomography, I would test sensitivity across receiver layouts and regularization choices more systematically. For validation, I would stratify results by season and atmospheric regime.

This research continues to shape how I approach product and program work: observe carefully, expose assumptions, compare independent evidence, and communicate the limits with the result.
