Project summary

From weather data to a defensible next step.

Weather interfaces are often good at displaying conditions and less good at helping someone compare risk, understand the source of a signal, or recognize when the available data cannot support a conclusion. RisQ explores that gap. It brings forecast stress, active NOAA/NWS alerts, regional ranking, side-by-side comparison, and a weather-aware assistant called SkyScout into one decision flow.

The project is also a public demonstration of how I combine domain knowledge, product judgment, and hands-on implementation. The aim was not to reproduce the sophistication of an operational forecasting center. It was to make a focused, inspectable product that respects public-data limitations and helps a user move from national awareness to a specific regional question.

Challenge

Make uncertainty legible without making the product timid.

A national weather map can create the illusion that every location is equally observed and every signal equally current. In practice, source coverage, refresh timing, forecast availability, and alert context differ. A useful product must still be decisive enough to support comparison while avoiding false precision.

The core design question became: how can the interface help a user notice where stress is emerging, inspect why an area is ranked, compare regions, and ask a natural-language question without allowing the system to invent missing evidence?

Constraints

Public data, heterogeneous signals, and a live interface.

  • Data availability: Public weather endpoints can be delayed, incomplete, rate-limited, or unavailable for a requested region.
  • Interpretation: Alerts, forecasts, and derived stress indicators represent different forms of evidence and should not be collapsed into one unexplained score.
  • Product honesty: SkyScout needed a path for saying that evidence was not available rather than completing a plausible-sounding answer.
  • Deployment: The product needed to remain lightweight enough for a public demo while handling loading, error, and empty states.

What I built

A layered decision workflow.

I structured the experience around progressive specificity. The map gives national orientation. Ranked regions direct attention. Comparison helps the user test whether one location is genuinely more stressed than another. Alert context provides authoritative evidence, while SkyScout turns the current product state into a conversational query surface.

I built the full-stack workflow with Node, Vite, and TypeScript, connected public weather sources, and designed state handling for data that is loading, available, partial, or unavailable. The assistant architecture is constrained by the evidence present in the product rather than treated as an independent oracle.

The product should be useful because it exposes the state of the evidence, not because it hides uncertainty behind a polished answer.

Outcome

A public, testable flagship.

RisQ is live, its source is public, and the work can be evaluated at three levels: the interface, the implementation, and the product choices behind both. It demonstrates map-based weather exploration, NOAA/NWS alert context, ranked regions, comparison workflows, and a weather-aware assistant in one coherent system.

For this portfolio, that matters more than a decorative prototype. Visitors can open the product, inspect the repository, and judge how the experience behaves when evidence is present or missing.

Methods

Product and technical approach.

  • Decision-flow mapping from national awareness to regional investigation.
  • Typed front-end and server-side interfaces with explicit state handling.
  • Public weather API integration and source-aware presentation.
  • Map-based visualization, ranking, comparison, and alert-context design.
  • Evidence-bounded assistant behavior and transparent unavailable states.
  • Iterative testing across desktop and mobile layouts.

Reflection

What I would improve next.

The next iteration would deepen provenance at the individual signal level, add clearer freshness indicators, and test ranking explanations with users who make weather-sensitive operational decisions. I would also formalize observability around upstream failures so reliability patterns become visible rather than anecdotal.

The broader lesson is durable: an AI-enabled decision product earns trust through boundaries, traceability, and recovery behavior as much as through the quality of its ideal-state answer.
