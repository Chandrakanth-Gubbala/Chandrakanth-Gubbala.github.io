Project summary

Turn a climate proposition into an operating model.

Residential electrification creates value across homeowners, contractors, technology providers, sponsors, utilities, and impact stakeholders. That same breadth makes planning difficult. Growth assumptions, product choices, installation capacity, partner quality, unit economics, and reporting logic can sit in separate spreadsheets without revealing how one decision affects another.

At Edge Energy, I built a five-year model to connect those parts. The work covered more than 80 product and operating categories, supported planning for growth from 200 to 900 homes annually, evaluated 103 potential partners, and linked homeowner outcomes to sponsor-facing ESG reporting.

Challenge

Create one decision view across a two-sided marketplace.

The business needed to coordinate homeowner demand with the supply, financing, installation, and sponsorship relationships that make a retrofit feasible. Scaling one side without the other would create bottlenecks. A purely financial forecast would miss operational capacity; an impact framework without economics would not support investment choices.

The decision question was therefore not simply, "How fast can the program grow?" It was, "What combination of categories, partners, economics, and operating capacity makes that growth credible?"

Constraints

Many categories, uneven evidence, shared economics.

  • Category complexity: More than 80 product and operating categories carried different adoption, margin, installation, and reporting implications.
  • Partner breadth: 103 candidates could not receive equal attention; the team needed explicit prioritization criteria.
  • Growth dependencies: Home volume, workforce capacity, partner throughput, and sponsor economics moved together.
  • Confidentiality: Client assumptions, pricing, and internal model sheets are not reproduced. The conceptual illustration contains no client data, logos, or fabricated metrics.

What I built

A model for choices, not just forecasts.

I organized the operating and financial logic into a five-year structure that let leadership test growth scenarios and see their implications across categories. Rather than leaving assumptions embedded in disconnected calculations, I made the major drivers explicit so the team could challenge them and compare alternatives.

I also designed a partner-evaluation framework using strategic fit, potential contribution, operating feasibility, and relationship value. That reduced a list of 103 organizations to 30 high-priority candidates. Finally, I developed an ESG framework that connected homeowner outcomes to sponsor-facing measures, making impact reporting part of the operating model rather than an after-the-fact narrative.

The most useful model was the one that made dependencies visible enough for executives to disagree productively.

Evidence

A public view of the model logic.

Decision layers represented in the work

  • Growth volume and operating capacity across a five-year horizon.
  • Product-category contribution and sequencing.
  • Partner prioritization from a broad candidate universe.
  • Homeowner value, sponsor economics, and ESG evidence.

Outcomes

A clearer path from ambition to execution.

  • Five-year operating and financial model spanning more than 80 categories.
  • Scenario planning for growth from 200 to 900 homes annually.
  • 30 priority partners identified from 103 candidates.
  • ESG logic linked to homeowner and sponsor outcomes.

These were planning outputs and decision structures rather than claims that every modeled scenario had already been realized. My contribution was the model architecture, analysis, prioritization framework, and executive translation used to examine those choices.

Methods

Strategic and analytical approach.

  • Driver-based operating and financial modeling.
  • Scenario analysis across growth, category, and capacity assumptions.
  • Partner scoring and prioritization against explicit criteria.
  • Unit-economics and ecosystem dependency mapping.
  • ESG measure design linked to operating events and stakeholder value.
  • Executive synthesis focused on decisions, sensitivities, and next actions.

Reflection

What I would improve now.

I would add a stronger uncertainty layer around the highest-leverage assumptions and connect the model to a lightweight operating dashboard so actual acquisition, installation, margin, and impact data could continuously recalibrate the plan. I would also make partner capacity constraints more dynamic rather than evaluating fit primarily at a fixed point in time.

The enduring lesson is that commercialization models become valuable when they connect money, capacity, relationships, and impact in the same conversation.
